Pick two lights, A and B. Every {filter} becomes a colour under A and, from the real spectra, a true colour under B. A chromatic adaptation transform (CAT) only gets the colour under A plus the white points of both lights, and has to predict the result under B. The gap between its prediction and the spectral truth is the error measured here.

How to read this tab

What a CAT does. It takes a colour as XYZ, the XYZ of the white under the old light and the XYZ of the white under the new light. It converts to a cone-like space, scales each of the three channels by new white divided by old white, and converts back. That is the idea you described.

Why there is an error. The CAT sees three numbers per colour. Two {filters} with different spectra can give the same XYZ under light A and different XYZ under light B. The CAT gives both the same answer, so it cannot be right for both.

What "true" means. The spectral result is {curveL} times the spectrum of light B, divided by the Y of white B. It is the colour relative to the white of light B, which is what a fully adapted viewer would see. That is the case a CAT with D = 1 tries to predict.

The two views. "One {filter}, all CATs" shows where each CAT lands compared with the true point. "All {filters}, one CAT" draws a whisker from every true point towards where the chosen CAT puts it. Whiskers are magnified so small errors become visible.

Degree of adaptation D

D in use1.000

Use D = 1 to judge how well a CAT predicts the spectral result. A smaller D under-adapts on purpose, so the error then mostly shows the incomplete adaptation.

Why a CAT cannot be exact

The {filter} and both lights are real spectra. The CAT sees them only through three broad, overlapping sensitivities (coloured, from the selected CAT's matrix). Spectra that give the same three numbers under A are indistinguishable to it, even when they split apart under B.
