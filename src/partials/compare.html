Advanced options and how it works

D, LA and the surround only apply to the “Matrix CAT” methods. The CRI and TM-30 methods use their standards’ fixed settings.

What “removing the white-point difference” means

Swapping light A for light B changes every colour for two reasons: the lights render each colour differently (what you want to see), and the whole scene gets warmer or cooler because the white points differ. Your eye mostly adapts to the second part. A white-point treatment takes it out, so what remains is the change in rendering. It works on light B’s colours by default: each is converted as if the eye had adapted from B’s white to A’s white, then compared with the same colour under A. B’s own white lands exactly on A’s white by construction.

  • Plain shift: subtract the white-point difference (in u′v′) from every colour. It treats all colours alike.
  • CRI (CIE 13.3): von Kries in CIE 1960 u,v. The c and d terms of each colour are scaled by (target white ÷ source white) and converted back. This is the 1995 CRI method, not a modern CAT.
  • TM-30 / CIECAM02: CAT02, a sharpened cone-like space, with complete adaptation (D = 1). TM-30 adapts both the test and reference light to the same white inside CIECAM02; because that adaptation is a per-channel scaling, comparing the two is the same as one CAT02 step from B’s white to A’s.
  • Matrix CATs (XYZ scaling, von Kries HPE, Bradford, CAT02, CAT16, Sharp, CMCCAT2000): the same recipe with a different matrix. D below 1 adapts only partly, leaving some of the white-point change in place. See the Adaptation lab tab to compare these against real spectra.

Colour and brightness together

Drag to rotate · wheel to zoom · click a dot to select

Vertical axis: lightness (how bright, relative to a white of the same light). Horizontal plane: colour, with grey in the middle and more saturated colours further out. Small dot = under A, larger dot = under B, joined by a line; with “Actual colour and brightness” each is drawn in the colour and brightness it really has (the bare light's white is full brightness, so filters that let little light through look dark), otherwise in the colour scale of the “Show” setting. The straight-line distance between the two dots is the apparent change ΔE: it combines a change in colour and a change in brightness in one number. A line that only goes up or down is a pure brightness change; one that only moves sideways is a pure colour change.

One filter, all lights

The other way round: pick one filter or pigment and see which lights in the library change it the least, or the most. Uses the white-point, brightness and colour-space settings above.

“Each light’s own ideal reference” compares the filter under every light with the same filter under a perfectly rendering light of the same colour temperature, so the ranking shows which lights show this filter most faithfully. With “light A” the ranking shows which lights are closest to (or furthest from) A. Click a column heading to sort by it, and click a light’s name to open it in the LED library. Colour dots show the colour the filter has under each light (left: reference, right: that light); in the brightness view they are darkened by how much light gets through.
